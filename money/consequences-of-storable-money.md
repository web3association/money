# Consequences of storable money

Money’s ability to preserve purchasing power provides individuals with financial security and allows payments to be transferred across time. Gesell nevertheless argued that conventional money combines durability, liquidity and general acceptability with unusually low carrying costs. This gives its holders an advantage over people offering goods and labour that cannot be retained as easily. According to Gesell, this initial asymmetry produces a chain of effects involving interest, investment, employment, aggregate demand, economic crises and the distribution of wealth.



## **The underlying monetary asymmetry**



**Money has an unfair advantage over goods**

Most commodities deteriorate, spoil, become obsolete or incur significant storage costs, whereas gold and fiat money can generally be retained for long periods at comparatively little cost. Money is not entirely costless to hold: gold requires secure storage, cash can be stolen and fiat currency can lose purchasing power through inflation. Nevertheless, money is ordinarily more durable and liquid than the goods against which it is exchanged. Sellers may therefore face pressure to exchange their goods before they lose value, while money holders usually have greater freedom to postpone a transaction. Gesell regarded this asymmetry as the central defect of conventional money.



**Money’s exchange and storage functions conflict**

Money is expected to function both as a medium of exchange and as a store of value, but these functions can create opposing incentives. As a medium of exchange, money must move between buyers and sellers so that goods and services can be traded. As a store of value, however, it is retained to preserve purchasing power for future use. Gesell therefore saw an inherent tension between monetary circulation and monetary storage: the first requires money to be transferred, while the second encourages its retention.



**Money can be withdrawn from circulation**

The owner of physical money can hoard it rather than spend or lend it. Because money is the generally accepted intermediary through which goods and labour are exchanged, widespread monetary retention can obstruct transactions that might otherwise take place. Goods may remain unsold, productive resources may remain unused and workers may remain unemployed even though corresponding needs still exist.

Saving should not automatically be equated with hoarding. Money deposited in a bank may finance lending, while money lent through financial markets becomes available to another party. Nevertheless, financial intermediation does not ensure that retained income will be converted into productive expenditure. Banks may decline to lend, potential borrowers may be unwilling to borrow or successive recipients may continue accumulating liquid balances. Gesell’s broader objection was that the circulation of money ultimately depends on the changing confidence, expectations and preferences of its holders rather than occurring at a reliable and predictable rate.



**Money holders acquire bargaining power**

The ability to postpone exchange gives money holders an advantage over people who cannot wait as easily. Producers face storage and operating costs, merchants hold inventories that may lose value, workers need income to meet living expenses and debtors must satisfy payment deadlines. Holders of liquid money, by contrast, can often delay a transaction without suffering an equivalent immediate loss.

Transactions may therefore be legally voluntary while the parties possess unequal capacities to wait. Money holders can refuse to purchase, lend or invest until they are offered sufficiently favourable terms. Gesell believed this withholding power enabled them to claim a portion of the proceeds of production as the condition for making their purchasing power available.



**Basic interest as the price of surrendering liquidity**

Gesell called the return arising from money’s withholding power basic interest or primeval interest, translated from the German _Urzins_. In his theory, basic interest was the payment money holders could demand for surrendering liquidity and allowing their purchasing power to be used by someone else. It was not, in his view, compensation for creating real wealth or directly providing a productive service.

Gesell distinguished basic interest from the other components of an observed lending rate. A market interest rate may include compensation for default risk, administrative expenses, expected inflation, the length of the loan and the lender’s provision of genuine financial services. His objection concerned the underlying return he believed money could command simply because its owner possessed the option to withhold it.

In Gesell’s theory, basic interest was therefore the monetary expression of money’s superior capacity to be withheld. It also established a minimum return that prospective productive investments would have to exceed.



## **Effects on investment and productive capital**



**Interest establishes an investment threshold**

Using Keynes’s later terminology, Gesell’s argument can be expressed by saying that the monetary rate of interest establishes a threshold that the expected marginal efficiency of capital must exceed. The marginal efficiency of capital is the expected rate of return from creating or acquiring an additional capital asset. It compares the asset’s present cost with the revenues it is expected to generate over its productive life. Examples include purchasing equipment, constructing a factory or expanding productive capacity.

An investor must compare the expected return from a productive project with the returns available from alternative uses of the same funds. Suppose, for example, that comparatively safe and liquid financial assets yield 3 per cent. A risky and illiquid productive investment would ordinarily need to offer more than 3 per cent to compensate for the possibility of failure, uncertainty about future revenue and the difficulty of recovering the funds before the project is completed. The relevant threshold is therefore not merely the base interest rate but the risk-adjusted opportunity cost of committing funds to productive investment.

Money itself should not be confused with an interest-bearing asset. Physical cash ordinarily pays no nominal interest, while government securities and bank accounts may produce a return because of the financial arrangements associated with them. Even so, Gesell believed that money’s liquidity and its holder’s ability to wait provided the foundation for the basic monetary component of interest. This basic interest was then transmitted to other financial assets and became part of the return against which productive investments were assessed.

Because the marginal efficiency of capital is an estimate of future revenues and costs, it cannot be known with certainty. Greater uncertainty generally increases the return investors require before committing funds.



**Reduced capital formation**

Projects whose expected risk-adjusted returns fall below this threshold will ordinarily not proceed. Factories may remain unbuilt, equipment may not be purchased and potentially valuable innovations may not be developed, even when they are technically feasible and socially useful.

Capital formation is also constrained by real factors, including access to resources, labour, technology, skills, energy and land, as well as expected consumer demand and regulatory conditions. Gesell’s claim was that monetary interest adds a separate financial constraint. By allowing wealth holders to retain liquid money or demand compensation before making purchasing power available, conventional money may prevent some otherwise viable productive investments from proceeding.

If money did not possess the same advantage as a durable and comparatively low-cost store of wealth, Gesell believed the monetary hurdle to investment would be lower. Capital formation could therefore be expected to continue beyond the point at which it presently stops, resulting in a larger stock of productive capital than would otherwise exist. This would not mean that investment would continue without limit, since risk, resource scarcity, depreciation and expected demand would still constrain it. The narrower claim is that removing basic interest would allow some investments with low but positive expected real returns to be undertaken.



**The scarcity of capital becomes self-perpetuating**

Gesell argued that returns on real capital tend to decline as capital becomes more abundant. Factories, machinery, housing and other assets can command relatively high returns when they are scarce, but additional investment expands productive capacity and increases competition among their owners. Once the expected return approaches the threshold established by monetary interest, however, further investment becomes unattractive. Capital formation therefore stops before capital becomes abundant enough to eliminate scarcity returns, allowing its relative scarcity and associated income to persist.

Rather than accept a lower return from an uncertain and illiquid productive project, wealth holders can retain liquidity or choose a safer financial alternative. The process can consequently become self-perpetuating: scarce capital earns a high return; new investment initially lowers that return; investment slows when the return approaches the monetary threshold; and capital remains scarce enough to continue producing income for its owners.

This is a theoretical claim rather than an uncontested economic law. Returns on capital may also persist because of risk, innovation, entrepreneurial skill, monopoly power, resource scarcity or continuing technological change. Gesell’s more specific contention was that basic monetary interest creates a floor beneath which expected returns on newly created capital cannot ordinarily fall without discouraging further private investment.



**Effects on labour demand and bargaining power**

If the monetary threshold prevents otherwise viable investment, fewer productive assets and opportunities may be created. Gesell expected this to weaken competition among employers and leave employment, productivity and wages lower than they otherwise would have been. The effect is not automatic: some investments complement workers, while automation may substitute machinery for particular forms of labour. Productive investment may nevertheless support employment indirectly by expanding output, creating new industries and generating additional income and demand.

Workers may also possess less bargaining power when productive assets and employment opportunities are scarce. When many workers compete for a limited number of jobs, employers can generally offer less favourable wages and conditions than they would have to offer if businesses were competing more intensely for labour. In this qualified sense, Gesell associated restricted capital formation with unemployment, economic dependence and a smaller share of production going to workers.



**Effects on production, competition and real prices**

Gesell also argued that restricted capital formation can leave productive capacity lower and goods and services more costly than they otherwise would be. Greater productive investment can introduce more efficient technology, expand output and enable new firms to compete with established producers. Improvements in productivity and competition may reduce unit production costs, improve quality and increase the quantity of goods available to consumers.

This outcome is not guaranteed. Additional investment may strengthen dominant firms, increase market concentration or flow into existing land and financial assets rather than the creation of productive capacity. Investments in automation can also produce benefits for owners without proportionately increasing employment. Even when production costs fall, firms with substantial market power may retain the gains as higher profits rather than pass them on to consumers.

Gesell’s argument is therefore strongest when stated conditionally: to the extent that monetary interest prevents productive investment and restricts effective competition, consumers may face higher real costs, lower quality or fewer choices than they would in a more capital-abundant economy. The benefit of greater capital formation would not necessarily appear as a fall in the overall nominal price level. Under a monetary policy that stabilised average prices, it might instead appear as higher real wages, greater output, improved products or lower relative prices in the industries experiencing greater investment.



## **Effects on circulation and macroeconomic stability**



**Velocity depends on confidence and expectations**

The effective level of monetary expenditure depends not only on the quantity of money but also on how frequently it is used. If people retain money for longer periods, the decline in its velocity can have an effect similar to a contraction of the actively circulating money supply. Businesses receive less revenue, inventories accumulate and production and employment may decline. If people begin spending the same stock of money more rapidly, the resulting increase in velocity can have an effect similar to monetary expansion, placing upward pressure on output or prices.

The willingness to retain money varies with confidence, expectations, interest rates and perceptions of risk. During prosperous periods, households and businesses may feel secure enough to spend and invest readily. During periods of uncertainty, households, businesses, banks and investors may all attempt to increase their liquid balances simultaneously. Although this may be prudent for each participant individually, the collective attempt to obtain liquidity can reduce expenditure precisely when the economy most needs demand. The same nominal quantity of money can therefore support substantially different levels of economic activity at different times.

Although savings held through financial institutions may be lent to others, financial intermediation does not guarantee that retained income will be converted into current consumption or productive investment. Gesell’s broader criticism was that monetary circulation remains dependent on the changing confidence and preferences of money holders rather than occurring at a reliable and predictable rate.



**Aggregate demand becomes unstable**

Expectations about money’s future purchasing power can alter the timing and composition of aggregate demand. Expected deflation may encourage consumers and businesses to postpone expenditure, while expected inflation may cause money holders to bring purchases forward or convert their balances into goods, property, securities, commodities or foreign currency.

Purchases of existing assets do not necessarily increase demand for newly produced goods, but they can raise asset prices, reduce demand for the domestic currency and affect subsequent spending behaviour. Expectations may therefore redirect expenditure as well as change its total level.

Moderate and predictable inflation may discourage the passive holding of cash, but it should not be said to discourage saving in general. People can continue saving by purchasing interest-bearing securities, equities, property or inflation-protected assets. High and unpredictable inflation may ultimately undermine demand by disrupting contracts, making economic calculation difficult and reducing confidence in the currency. If depreciation becomes sufficiently severe, people may shorten contracts, demand payment in alternative assets or abandon the currency in favour of foreign money or barter.

Inflation and deflation are not automatically self-reinforcing in every case. Monetary and fiscal policy, production, credit conditions, wage formation and public expectations can interrupt either process. The broader Gesellian criticism is that conventional money makes current demand unusually sensitive to expectations about future purchasing power. Anticipated deflation may encourage postponed expenditure, while anticipated inflation may encourage accelerated or speculative expenditure.



**Deflation rewards monetary retention**

When the general price level falls, a fixed quantity of money gains purchasing power. Even if cash pays no nominal interest, it produces a positive real return because it will purchase more goods and services in the future. Holding money may consequently appear more attractive than spending it or committing it to an uncertain long-term investment.

This creates the possibility of a reinforcing process. An initial decline in demand causes prices to fall; falling prices increase the real return from retaining money; and the resulting postponement of expenditure reduces demand further. Businesses expecting lower future prices may also reduce production and investment because they anticipate receiving less revenue for their products. Gesell regarded this reward for monetary retention as one of the most destructive consequences of conventional money.

Deflation does not mean that every individual will postpone every purchase. Essential consumption must continue, and some buyers may take advantage of lower prices. The strength of the effect depends on expectations, debt levels, income security and whether people believe the decline in prices will persist. Gesell’s argument concerns the additional incentive that deflation creates to delay discretionary expenditure and investment, not an absolute cessation of exchange.



**Economic crises and cumulative contraction**

These mechanisms can combine to turn an initial deterioration in confidence into a cumulative contraction. Attempts by households, businesses and banks to increase liquidity reduce velocity and aggregate expenditure. Lower business revenue leads to reductions in production, investment and employment, which further depress income and demand. Falling prices may then reward additional monetary retention and increase real debt burdens, while defaults and declining collateral values weaken the financial system.

During periods of optimism, faster spending, greater borrowing and credit expansion may produce the opposite dynamic. Rising income and asset prices may reinforce confidence and encourage further borrowing and investment. If expectations later reverse, the movement toward liquidity can be sudden and destabilising.

Gesell treated money’s ability to be withheld as a fundamental source of this instability. A more qualified interpretation is that monetary storability and liquidity preference provide one mechanism through which crises can develop and intensify. Supply disruptions, excessive leverage, banking failures, policy errors, wars and other shocks can also cause or worsen economic contractions. The Gesellian claim is not that storable money is the sole cause of every crisis, but that widespread monetary retention can allow an initial disturbance to develop into a broader collapse of circulation, demand and employment.



## **Distributional and political consequences**



**Interest contributes to cumulative inequality**

Because interest transfers income from borrowers and users of capital to creditors and owners of financial assets, Gesell believed it could contribute to the concentration of income and property. Those possessing net monetary wealth can receive a continuing income stream, while borrowers must generate enough income to repay both principal and interest.

The distributional effect is not as simple as a direct transfer from poor borrowers to rich lenders. Borrowers may include wealthy households, profitable corporations and governments, while creditors may include ordinary savers, pension funds and insurance schemes. Taxes, consumption, defaults, inflation and investment losses can also prevent wealth from compounding indefinitely. Gesell’s distributional objection concerned basic interest specifically, not compensation for risk, administration or genuine financial services.

Nevertheless, ownership of net interest-bearing assets is generally concentrated among wealthier households and institutions. People with few assets may receive some interest indirectly through savings or pensions while simultaneously paying interest through mortgages, consumer debts, rents, taxes and the prices charged by indebted businesses. Gesell’s structural claim was therefore that basic interest tends to favour those who possess more financial wealth than debt, even though particular creditors and debtors do not correspond neatly to separate social classes.

When interest income is reinvested, economic inequality may become self-reinforcing. Greater monetary wealth enables its owners to acquire additional income-producing assets, while the income from those assets makes further accumulation possible. Those without significant assets remain dependent primarily on wages and may need to borrow to purchase housing, education or productive equipment. In Gesell’s account, the monetary system can therefore transform an initial inequality of wealth into a persistent inequality of income and economic power.



**Concentrated liquidity produces economic and political power**

The unequal capacity to postpone exchange means that transactions may be legally voluntary without occurring between parties possessing equal economic freedom. Workers, debtors and producers often face immediate financial obligations, while holders of liquid wealth may be able to wait for more favourable terms. Having established this bargaining advantage, Gesell argued that its repeated exercise could deepen the dependence of those without substantial monetary reserves.

If monetary wealth becomes concentrated, its owners may also gain disproportionate influence over investment, employment and access to credit. Their decisions about whether and where to make finance available can shape which businesses expand, which projects proceed and which workers obtain employment. Concentrated economic resources may also be converted into political influence through ownership of institutions, lobbying, campaign finance or control over public communication. These wider political effects are not produced by monetary storability alone, but they may reinforce the economic inequalities associated with basic interest.



## **Why conventional remedies are insufficient**



**Legal restrictions on interest do not remove its cause**

If basic interest arises from the ability to withhold money, a legal ceiling does not eliminate the underlying bargaining advantage. Lenders may refuse to lend when the permitted return is insufficient, move funds into alternative markets, impose fees, demand greater collateral or conceal interest within other contractual terms. Riskier borrowers may consequently be excluded from formal credit.

This does not mean that interest-rate regulations are always ineffective or undesirable. Well-designed restrictions may protect borrowers from exploitative practices, improve transparency and prevent extreme charges arising from desperation or limited competition. Gesell’s narrower point was that regulating the visible interest rate does not alter the monetary conditions from which he believed basic interest arose. If money holders retain the option not to lend, they continue to possess bargaining power even when the price they may legally charge is restricted.

Gesell therefore concluded that interest ceilings could regulate lending practices without eliminating basic interest. In his theory, the incentive to withhold money itself had to be changed, while compensation for risk, administration and genuine financial services could remain.



**Creating more money does not guarantee circulation**

Increasing the quantity of money does not ensure that the additional balances will finance consumption or productive investment. Recipients may retain them as liquidity, banks may hold additional reserves and investors may purchase existing assets instead of financing new productive capacity. The result depends on how the money enters the economy and on the income, debts, confidence and expectations of its recipients.

During a contraction, additional money may accommodate an increased demand for liquidity without restoring spending. Banks may remain unwilling to lend, businesses may refuse to borrow and households may repay debts or build precautionary balances. If confidence later returns and retained balances begin circulating more rapidly, however, the enlarged money supply may place upward pressure on prices.

Gesell therefore believed that monetary expansion alone left the underlying option to retain money unchanged. He proposed combining active management of the money supply with a carrying charge intended to encourage circulation. The monetary authority would adjust the quantity of money to stabilise average prices, while the carrying charge would discourage prolonged retention. Whether such a system would achieve its intended results remains disputed, but it followed directly from Gesell’s diagnosis: increasing the quantity of money without changing the incentive to withhold it does not remove the underlying problem.
