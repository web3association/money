# Separating store of value & storability

In Philip Pye’s English translation of _The Natural Economic Order_, Gesell writes:

> _**“Money is an instrument of exchange and nothing else”**_

Gesell did not deny that people need to save. What he denied is that idle money itself should be the ideal vehicle for saving. His distinction was approximately:

* **Saving** - Setting aside part of one’s present income or wealth for future use. The resulting savings may be held as money, lent to others, or invested in real or financial assets.
* **Hoarding money** - Maintaining unusually large or prolonged idle monetary balances rather than spending, lending or investing them, thereby reducing the rate at which money circulates.

He thought that durable money which was inexpensive to store gave its owner a special advantage over owners of goods. Potatoes rot, buildings require upkeep and merchandise costs money to store, whereas conventional money can wait and be left idle. Consequently, its owner can refuse to buy, lend or invest until offered interest or better prices.

His proposal was therefore not to abolish saving, but to make the currency bear a small carrying cost. People could still preserve wealth through deposits, loans, buildings, equipment and other claims on productive assets, but they would have less incentive to hold excessive monetary balances as a means of preserving and accumulating wealth.



## Understanding the store of value function and the storability of money

Silvio Gesell also writes, “the power of money to effect exchanges, its technical quality from the mercantile standpoint, is in inverse proportion to its technical quality from the banking standpoint”.

By the “mercantile standpoint”, Gesell meant money’s effectiveness in facilitating exchange. By the “banking standpoint”, he meant its suitability for being retained as a liquid reserve or financial asset. His claim was that the qualities which make money attractive to hold can also make it easier to withhold from exchange.

This statement requires further thought because Gesell discussed both saving (preserving wealth for future use) and hoarding money (holding the medium of exchange idle). Money’s store of value function and its low-cost storability are related but conceptually distinct.

A store of value is an asset’s ability to preserve purchasing power over time. It allows someone to receive value now and use that value later. Money performs this function when it can be saved and subsequently exchanged for goods and services without losing too much purchasing power in the future. A store of value doesn’t need to preserve value perfectly. Inflation, risk and other costs may somewhat reduce the asset’s future purchasing power. These factors can make an asset less desirable as a store of value, but they do not necessarily prevent the asset from being a useful store of value. The purpose of a store of value is ultimately to transfer a meaningful amount of value from the present into the future.

Storable money is money that can be held for future use without physically deteriorating, expiring or incurring substantial carrying costs.

For the purposes of this discussion, the distinction can be stated simply:

* **Store of value** - How well money preserves purchasing power through time.
* **Storability** - How easily and inexpensively a monetary balance can be held through time.



## Store of value and storability examples

Gold can function as a store of value because it is durable, scarce and widely valued, although its purchasing power can still fluctuate. It is also physically storable: its durability and high value relative to its size allow substantial wealth to be held in a compact form. From Gesell’s perspective, this combination can be problematic because gold can be withdrawn from circulation and retained for long periods without imposing a significant cost on its owner.

Now let’s consider Gesell’s stamped-money system. Provided that the currency’s purchasing power remained reasonably stable, it could continue to transfer a meaningful amount of value from the present into the future. Nevertheless, anyone holding the currency would have to purchase stamps periodically to maintain its full nominal value. The currency could therefore remain a reasonably effective store of purchasing power while becoming costly to hold idle. Demurrage would not make the money literally impossible to store; rather, it would remove the advantage of storing it indefinitely at little or no cost.

The same principle could theoretically be applied to gold. Imagine a monetary system in which everyone’s gold holdings were recorded and periodically taxed. The authorities could collect a small percentage of each person’s gold at regular intervals. Although such a system would be administratively difficult, particularly if people could conceal their gold, it illustrates that demurrage does not necessarily depend on the physical deterioration of the monetary asset. A durable asset suitable as a store of value can therefore still bear an ongoing carrying cost if that cost is imposed institutionally by the monetary system.

These examples show why the store of value function and storability should not be treated as identical. The purchasing power of a monetary unit might remain stable even while holding a balance of those units entails a recurring cost. Conversely, an asset may be easy to store physically while failing to preserve purchasing power. What matters for Gesell’s proposal is not simply whether money retains value, or in some cases even increases in value, but whether its owner can withhold it from circulation indefinitely without cost.



## Low-cost storability as the foundational problem with money

From this perspective, money’s ability to function as a store of value is not necessarily the foundational problem to resolve. The deeper problem, in Gesell’s analysis, is money’s comparatively low-cost storability: it can often be held for prolonged periods at a lower carrying cost than ordinary goods. This gives holders of money the option to wait, while producers and sellers may bear depreciation, maintenance and storage expenses on the goods they hold.

Demurrage addresses this asymmetry by making prolonged possession of money costly. Money remains storable in a literal sense, but it is no longer costless to store. Once an ongoing charge applies to monetary balances, the changes in the currency’s purchasing power become a separate issue. Money could remain stable in value, or even appreciate to some degree, and still be subject to an incentive to circulate, provided that the demurrage charge was sufficient to offset the benefit of holding it.

What matters overall is the expected net return from holding money. If the currency appreciated by less than the demurrage rate, its holder would still face a net carrying cost. If its expected appreciation exceeded the demurrage rate, however, holding it could remain attractive despite the charge. Demurrage would therefore have to be sufficiently high to offset much of or all of the expected gain from appreciation if it were to maintain the intended incentive to circulate.

It is therefore unnecessary to make the currency itself lose purchasing power through inflation or by using a form of money that deteriorates in order to achieve Gesell’s desired circulation effect. Deliberately reducing purchasing power would discourage hoarding, but it would also weaken money’s store of value function and could reduce people’s willingness to accept it. A more targeted approach would be to preserve reasonably stable purchasing power while imposing demurrage directly on monetary balances, thereby increasing the cost of leaving them idle.

Extending Gesell’s proposal, the store of value quality of money and a variable rate of demurrage could operate as counterweights. Price stability or appreciation would tend to increase the demand to hold money, while demurrage would tend to reduce that demand and encourage circulation. If demand for idle balances became excessive, the demurrage rate could be increased; if the demand to hold money became too weak, it could be reduced. The central objective would not be to prevent money from preserving value, but to prevent it from being withheld from circulation indefinitely without cost.

Gesell’s claim that “money is an instrument of exchange and nothing else” therefore appears too absolute. Money can serve as both a medium of exchange and a store of value. The ability to preserve purchasing power is beneficial for it to function effectively in exchange. From this perspective, the problem is not value preservation itself, but the possibility that money can be held idle for prolonged periods at an insufficient carrying cost. Demurrage seeks to address that problem by imposing a negative net return on monetary balances without necessarily destabilising the purchasing power of the monetary unit.
