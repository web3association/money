# Money: definition, functions and forms

A medium of exchange solves the coincidence-of-wants problem inherent in barter. In a barter system, two parties must each possess something the other wants, find one another and agree to exchange their goods or services in corresponding amounts. A generally accepted medium of exchange removes this requirement by introducing an intermediate step between selling and buying.

Instead of exchanging apples directly for milk, Alice can sell her apples for money and later use that money to purchase milk or anything else she needs. Bob can sell his milk for money without having to find someone who both wants milk and possesses the particular good Bob wants in return. The wants of the participants no longer need to coincide at the same place and time.

By separating individual exchanges in this way, money greatly expands the scale and scope of mutually beneficial economic activity.



## The separation of buying and selling

There is an important difference between barter and monetary exchange. In barter, buying and selling are two parts of the same action. A person cannot sell one good without simultaneously receiving another good or service in return. The value offered and the value received are directly connected within the same transaction.

This principle is related to what is commonly known in economics as Say’s Law. Its simplest formulation is that “supply creates its own demand”. By producing goods and services for exchange, producers also create purchasing power with which other goods and services can be acquired.

The introduction of money changes this relationship. Because money acts as an intermediate step, selling and buying become separate actions. A person can sell something today, retain the money and postpone purchasing something else until a later date.

This separation is one of money’s most useful characteristics. It allows exchange to take place between people who do not have directly corresponding wants, and it allows purchasing power to be transferred across time. However, it also makes it possible for money received from a sale not to return immediately to circulation. Someone can sell without subsequently buying, lending or investing. The implications of retaining money in this way will become important later in this analysis.



## Functions of money

Money is commonly associated with three functions which include being a medium of exchange, unit of account and store of value. These functions are closely related, but each describes a different way in which money can be used.



**Medium of exchange**

Money functions as a medium of exchange when it acts as an intermediary between the sale and purchase of goods and services. People accept money because they expect that other people will also accept it in future exchanges.

General acceptance is therefore central to this function. An asset cannot operate effectively as a medium of exchange if each recipient must first be persuaded that it has value or if it can only be used with a small number of participants.

A widely accepted medium of exchange reduces the time and effort required to trade. It allows people to specialise in particular forms of production while using the income they receive to acquire the many other things they need.



**Unit of account**

A unit of account is a standard measure used to express and compare the prices of different goods and services. It gives participants in an economy a common language for measuring economic value.

If gold is used as the medium of exchange, its unit of account might be an ounce or gram of gold. If corn is used, the unit might be a pound or bushel of corn. Modern currencies use units such as pounds, dollars or euros.

The unit-of-account function is closely connected to the medium-of-exchange function. The unit of account is usually the standard increment used to count the instrument exchanged as money. A shared unit makes it easier to compare prices, record obligations and determine how much money should be transferred in a transaction.

It is therefore difficult to imagine a widely used medium of exchange that does not also provide, or become associated with, a unit of account. For this reason, the two functions can sometimes be treated as parts of the same broader exchange function.



**Store of value**

Money also enables purchasing power to be transferred from the present into the future. Someone can receive money today, retain it and use it to purchase goods or services later. In this role, money functions as a store of value.

A store of value does not have to preserve purchasing power perfectly. Inflation, risk, fees and changes in market conditions may reduce how much an asset can purchase in the future. An asset can nevertheless remain a useful store of value if it preserves a meaningful proportion of its purchasing power.

The ability to transfer value through time is useful for individuals and businesses. It makes it possible to retain earnings, prepare for future expenses and delay a purchase without having to hold the particular goods that will eventually be consumed.

However, money’s store-of-value function must be distinguished from its storability. Store of value describes how effectively money preserves purchasing power. Storability describes how easily and inexpensively a monetary balance can be retained. These ideas are related but are not identical.



## What defines money?

Money can be understood primarily through its use and acceptance. An instrument functions as money when it is generally accepted as payment for goods and services and can therefore serve as a medium of exchange.

Legal-tender status is another characteristic commonly associated with money. Legal tender is money that has been formally recognised by a government for the settlement of monetary obligations. General acceptance and legal-tender status often coincide, particularly in national monetary systems, but they are not the same thing.

A form of money can be legally designated without being willingly accepted in every practical exchange. Conversely, an instrument may be widely accepted within a community even though it does not have formal legal-tender status.

New forms of money are currently being explored in contexts including Web3 networks, alternative exchange systems, community currencies and complementary currencies. Many do not have legal-tender status, but this does not necessarily prevent them from achieving acceptance as a means of payment.

Money should therefore not be defined solely by its physical form, its commodity backing or the institution that issues it. Its ability to perform monetary functions, especially its acceptance as a medium of exchange, is fundamental.



## Forms of money

Money has taken several forms throughout history. Three important categories are commodity money, representative money and fiat money.



## **Commodity money**

Commodity money is money made from, or directly represented by, a commodity that is considered valuable independently of its monetary use. Historical examples include gold, silver, copper, shells and salt.

Producing commodity money may involve extracting, mining, processing, transporting and standardising the underlying material. Precious metals, for example, must be mined and refined before they can be minted into coins or otherwise used in exchange.

The value of commodity money is linked to the material from which it is made. This can support confidence in the money, but it also introduces several limitations.



**Problems with commodity money**

* **Low-cost storability** - Some forms of commodity money, particularly precious metals such as gold and silver, can be retained for long periods without physically deteriorating. This makes them attractive to hold but also makes it possible to withdraw money from circulation for extended periods.
* **Rigidity of supply** - The supply of commodity money is tied to the physical availability and extraction of the underlying commodity. Its supply may therefore be unable to respond easily to changes in economic activity or demand for money.
* **Imbalance of bargaining power** - Durable commodity money may give its holder more freedom to postpone an exchange than a seller holding goods that deteriorate or incur storage costs. A holder of gold can often wait, while a seller of food, labour or other time-sensitive goods may face increasing pressure to transact.
* **High production costs** - Mining, refining, transporting and minting precious metals can require substantial amounts of labour, energy and other resources.
* **Storage and transport costs** - Physical commodities can be heavy, bulky or expensive to secure. Large transfers may be difficult to conduct efficiently.
* **Market fluctuations** - The monetary value of commodity money can change with supply and demand for the underlying commodity. Its purchasing power may therefore fluctuate even when its physical quantity remains unchanged.



## **Representative money**

Representative money is an instrument that represents a claim on an underlying commodity or asset. It can be redeemed for a specified amount of whatever provides its backing.

Gold and silver certificates are common historical examples. Instead of transferring the commodity itself, participants exchange certificates representing claims on commodities held in reserve.

This makes exchange more convenient because paper or ledger-based claims can be easier to divide, store and transport than the underlying commodity. However, representative money depends on the institutions responsible for holding the reserves and honouring redemption.



**Problems with representative money**

* **It inherits problems from commodity money** - Because representative money is a claim on an underlying commodity, it can inherit limitations associated with that commodity, including supply rigidity and fluctuations in value.
* **Vulnerability of the backing -** The reserves may be lost, stolen, mismanaged or misrepresented. Certificates can also be issued in quantities that exceed the assets available for redemption.
* **Dependence on trust and confidence** - Users must trust that the institution managing the reserves possesses the assets it claims to hold and will honour redemption requests. A loss of confidence can destabilise the currency.
* **Risk of manipulation** - The relationship between the representative money and its backing can be changed. Institutions or governments may alter the redemption terms or reduce the proportion of reserves held against the outstanding money.
* **Management complexity** - Maintaining, protecting, auditing and redeeming reserves can be administratively complex and expensive.
* **Limited control over supply** - The quantity of representative money that can be issued is constrained by the availability of its backing asset. This may limit the system’s ability to adjust the money supply in response to changing economic conditions.



## Fiat money

Fiat money is money that is not redeemable for a fixed quantity of a physical commodity. Its monetary value depends primarily on public acceptance, institutional trust and the legal and administrative systems that support its use.

Modern national currencies are predominantly fiat currencies. They can exist as banknotes and coins, but most fiat money is held and transferred digitally through bank accounts and payment systems.

In modern fiat systems, money is created through the activities of central banks and commercial banks. Central banks issue physical currency and create central-bank reserves. Commercial banks create deposit money when they make loans, crediting the borrower’s account with a new bank deposit.

This allows the money supply to respond more flexibly to economic activity than under a strictly commodity-backed system. However, it also gives monetary authorities and the banking system substantial influence over the quantity, allocation and conditions of money creation.



**Problems with fiat money**

* **Inflation and over-issuance** - Because fiat money is not limited by the availability of a physical commodity, governments and banking systems can create additional money. If the money supply grows faster than the availability of goods and services, the currency’s purchasing power may decline. In extreme cases, persistent over-issuance and loss of confidence can contribute to very high inflation or hyperinflation.
* **Dependence on institutional trust** - Fiat money has no fixed redemption value in an underlying commodity. Its use depends on confidence in the institutions, laws and economic systems that support it. A serious loss of trust can weaken acceptance of the currency.
* **Interest and debt cycles** - A large proportion of fiat money is created through bank lending. Borrowers must repay principal as well as the financing charges associated with their loans. This system can contribute to growing levels of debt and can encourage the accumulation of interest-bearing financial assets.
* **Potential for abuse** - The ability to create and allocate money can be used irresponsibly. Poor monetary, fiscal or banking decisions can contribute to inflation, financial instability, asset-price distortions and the concentration of economic power.
